Biology Revision Notes: Cells, Respiration and Photosynthesis

The cell is the basic unit of all living organisms. Prokaryotic cells, found in
bacteria and archaea, have no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles; their genetic
material sits free in the cytoplasm in a region called the nucleoid. Eukaryotic cells,
found in plants, animals, fungi and protists, keep their DNA inside a membrane-bound
nucleus and contain specialised organelles.

Mitochondria are the organelles responsible for aerobic respiration. They have a
double membrane, and the inner membrane is folded into structures called cristae,
which increase the surface area available for the reactions that produce ATP. ATP,
adenosine triphosphate, is the molecule cells use to store and transfer chemical
energy. Aerobic respiration converts glucose and oxygen into carbon dioxide and water,
releasing roughly 38 molecules of ATP per molecule of glucose.

When oxygen is scarce, cells switch to anaerobic respiration. In animal muscle this
produces lactic acid and only two molecules of ATP per glucose. In yeast the same
shortage produces ethanol and carbon dioxide, a process called fermentation, which is
what makes bread rise and beer ferment.

Chloroplasts carry out photosynthesis in plant cells and in algae. They contain the
green pigment chlorophyll, which absorbs light energy, mostly in the red and blue
parts of the spectrum, and reflects green light, which is why leaves look green.
Photosynthesis uses light energy to convert carbon dioxide and water into glucose and
oxygen. The reactions take place in two stages: the light-dependent reactions in the
thylakoid membranes, and the light-independent reactions, also called the Calvin
cycle, in the stroma.

The rate of photosynthesis is affected by three limiting factors: light intensity,
carbon dioxide concentration and temperature. A limiting factor is whichever of these
is in shortest supply, because it alone caps the rate no matter how plentiful the
others are. Temperature acts differently from the other two: raising it speeds the
reactions only until the enzymes involved begin to denature, after which the rate
falls sharply.

Osmosis is the movement of water molecules from a dilute solution to a concentrated
solution across a partially permeable membrane. Diffusion is the net movement of
particles from a region of high concentration to a region of low concentration, and
needs no energy. Active transport moves substances against the concentration gradient
and therefore requires energy supplied by ATP.

Enzymes are biological catalysts made of protein. They speed up reactions by lowering
the activation energy required, and each enzyme has an active site whose shape is
complementary to its specific substrate. Extremes of temperature or pH change the
shape of the active site permanently, a change called denaturation, after which the
substrate no longer fits and the enzyme stops working.
